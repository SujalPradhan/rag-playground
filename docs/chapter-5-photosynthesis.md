# Chapter 5: Photosynthesis — Lecture Notes

## 5.1 Overview
Photosynthesis is the process by which plants, algae and some bacteria use light energy to turn carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen. It takes place mainly in the leaves, inside organelles called chloroplasts.

The overall equation is:
6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 + 6 O2

In words: six molecules of carbon dioxide and six molecules of water, using light energy, make one molecule of glucose and six molecules of oxygen.

## 5.2 The chloroplast
A chloroplast has a double outer membrane. Inside are stacks of flattened discs called thylakoids; a stack is called a granum. The fluid around the thylakoids is the stroma. The green pigment chlorophyll sits in the thylakoid membranes and absorbs mostly red and blue light, reflecting green light, which is why leaves look green.

## 5.3 Stage one: the light-dependent reactions
The light-dependent reactions happen in the thylakoid membranes and need light directly.
1. Chlorophyll absorbs light, which excites electrons.
2. Water molecules are split (photolysis), releasing oxygen as a by-product, plus hydrogen ions and electrons.
3. The energy from the excited electrons is used to make two energy-carrier molecules: ATP and NADPH.

The oxygen we breathe comes from the splitting of water in this stage, not from carbon dioxide.

## 5.4 Stage two: the Calvin cycle
The Calvin cycle (also called the light-independent reactions) happens in the stroma. It does not need light directly, but it depends on the ATP and NADPH made in stage one.
1. Carbon fixation: the enzyme RuBisCO attaches carbon dioxide to a five-carbon sugar called RuBP.
2. Reduction: ATP and NADPH are used to turn the products into a three-carbon sugar, G3P.
3. Regeneration: some G3P leaves the cycle to build glucose; the rest is recycled to regenerate RuBP.

RuBisCO is thought to be the most abundant protein on Earth.

## 5.5 Factors that affect the rate of photosynthesis
- Light intensity: more light increases the rate, until another factor becomes limiting.
- Carbon dioxide concentration: more CO2 increases the rate, up to a point.
- Temperature: enzymes work faster as temperature rises, but above about 35–40 °C they begin to denature and the rate falls.
- Water availability: when water is short, leaves close their stomata, which also limits CO2 intake.

The factor in shortest supply is called the limiting factor.

## 5.6 Why it matters
Photosynthesis is the starting point of almost every food chain, and it produces the oxygen most living things need. It also removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, which links it to climate change discussed in Week 11.

## 5.7 Common exam mistakes
- Saying the oxygen comes from carbon dioxide. It comes from water.
- Saying the Calvin cycle happens "at night". It can happen in daylight; it just doesn't use light directly.
- Confusing photosynthesis with respiration. Respiration releases energy from glucose; photosynthesis stores energy in glucose.
